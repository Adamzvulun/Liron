## The Silent Beacon

74 steps in 10 chapters.

### Chapter 1: Embers

The Signal Tower has been dark for forty winters. Its beacon once answered ships, caravans and storms; now pigeons nest in the lantern room. The Guildmaster hands you a pair of brass headphones. “The tower doesn’t need eyes,” she says. “It needs ears. Every sound you learn lights a lamp on the stair. Light enough of them, and the beacon may remember how to speak.”

1. **The First Two Sounds.**
2. **Lamp U.** Dust lifts from the steps as the third lamp wakes. On the landing lies the last operator’s logbook, its final entry unfinished.
3. **Lamp R.** The fourth lamp shows a brass key on the sill, warm, as if someone had only just set it down.
4. **The Warm Key.** The brass key is warm under your fingers. You press, and far below a relay clicks in answer. The Guildmaster, passing with her ledger, stops for the length of one letter.
5. **Lamp E.** The shortest sound of all, and the brightest lamp yet. The pigeons leave the lantern room.
6. **Before the Lamp Fades.** A lamp flares, you name it, and it holds a moment longer. “Good,” says the Guildmaster. “Don’t count the sounds. Know them.”
7. **Lamp S.** Six lamps. Out on the water, a fishing boat flashes its deck light at the tower. Coincidence, surely.
8. **Lamp N.** The seventh lamp lights a door marked RADIO ROOM. It is locked from the inside.
9. **Lamp A.** Eight lamps burn on the stair. The Guildmaster closes her ledger. “The Gatekeeper will want a word with you.”
10. **Trial: The Gatekeeper.** The gate swings open onto the upper stair. For the first time in forty winters, a faint glow shows in the lantern room, and down in the square, people stop to look up.

### Chapter 2: The Upper Stair

Above the gate the stair turns cold and narrow. The logbook speaks of “the far voice”: a station that kept calling the tower long after it went dark.

11. **The Logbook’s Words.** Past the gate stands a shelf of codebooks, swollen with damp. The first page you open is a list of short words, each ticked in the last operator’s hand. You tick the first one yourself.
12. **Lamp P.** The ninth lamp, and a draft from a broken pane. You stop the gap with a page from the logbook’s empty back.
13. **Lamp T.** The tenth lamp. The next page of the logbook is dated the night the beacon failed.
14. **Lamp L.** Lamp eleven shows a callsign scratched into the rail, half worn away by forty winters of hands.
15. **Lamp W.** Twelve lamps. Music drifts up from the Caravanserai; the city keeps later hours now that the tower glows.
16. **A Steadier Hand.** Your dits have stopped tripping over your dahs. The relay below keeps time with you now.
17. **Lamp I.** The thirteenth lamp. In a hurried hand, the logbook says: “The far voice is getting weaker. I will answer tomorrow.”
18. **Lamp for the period (.).** The full stop lights the fourteenth lamp. Sentences can end now, and so can messages.
19. **Lamp J.** Lamp fifteen. The locked radio room hums whenever you pass its door.
20. **Lamp Z.** Sixteen lamps. At the top of the stair waits a second gate, older than the first, with a speaking grille.
21. **Trial: The Warden of the Stair.** The second gate opens. The lantern room is close now, and somewhere in the static you are sure you heard someone call the tower by name.

### Chapter 3: The Lantern Room

Beyond the second gate, a ladder climbs into the lantern room. The great lens is cracked and gray with salt, and beside it sits the last operator’s chair, turned toward the window as if waiting.

22. **Lamp for the equals sign (=).** A break sign lights the seventeenth lamp: the pause between one thought and the next. The logbook uses it on every page.
23. **Lamp F.** Lamp eighteen. The operator’s chair creaks as you pass, though nobody is in it.
24. **Lamp O.** Nineteen lamps. The cracked lens throws their light in pieces across the walls.
25. **Lamp Y.** The twentieth lamp. Taped inside the logbook’s cover: a list of stations the tower once spoke to, most of them crossed out.
26. **The Operator’s Chair.** You sit in the last operator’s chair to key. This time it doesn’t creak.
27. **Lamp for the comma (,).** A comma for the twenty-first lamp. Messages can breathe now.
28. **Lamp V.** Lamp twenty-two. The window glass hums when the wind comes off the sea, like a carrier with nobody on it.
29. **Lamp G.** Twenty-three lamps. The Guildmaster climbs up to see for herself, and stays longer than she meant to.
30. **Lamp 5.** The first number, and the twenty-fourth lamp. The logbook’s last pages are full of them: times, bearings, signal strengths.
31. **The List of Stations.** You read the crossed-out list aloud, call by call, as fast as you can copy them. One call near the bottom was never crossed out.
32. **Trial: The Keeper of the Lens.** The lens turns a quarter-turn on its old bearings and catches the evening light. Out on the water, three boats answer at once.

### Chapter 4: The Far Voice

With the lens turning, the tower can listen farther than ever. Each night, at the same hour, something faint comes through the static: the far voice from the logbook, still calling.

33. **Lamp for the slash (/).** A slash for lamp twenty-five. It splits callsigns from where they’re sent: the far voice is somewhere else tonight.
34. **Lamp Q.** The twenty-sixth lamp. Q means a question in the operator’s shorthand, and the logbook is full of them.
35. **Lamp 9.** Lamp twenty-seven. You write down the hour the far voice comes: always nine minutes past.
36. **Lamp 2.** Twenty-eight lamps. The far voice sends two letters, again and again. They might be initials.
37. **Nine Minutes Past.** At nine minutes past you key the tower’s call into the dark, though nothing is connected yet. The Guildmaster pretends not to hear.
38. **Lamp H.** The twenty-ninth lamp. Down in the square, the night watch has taken to looking up at the tower when it chimes.
39. **Lamp 3.** Lamp thirty. The logbook’s author signed off with “73”: best regards, in the old shorthand.
40. **Lamp 8.** Thirty-one lamps. The far voice is stronger tonight. Someone is turning toward you, too.
41. **Lamp B.** The thirty-second lamp. The radio room door is warm to the touch, and its lock has rusted through.
42. **Trial: The Storm Front.** The storm passes. In the quiet after it, the far voice sends one clear line: the tower’s own callsign, and then a question mark.

### Chapter 5: The Beacon Speaks

One flight of stair is left in the dark, and with it the last sounds an operator needs. When they’re lit, the beacon can answer whoever is calling.

43. **Lamp for the question mark (?).** A question mark for lamp thirty-three, the sign the far voice ends with every night.
44. **Lamp 4.** Thirty-four lamps. A child in the square points at the tower and counts them out loud.
45. **Lamp 7.** Lamp thirty-five. You can send “73” yourself now, the operator’s goodbye.
46. **Lamp C.** The thirty-sixth lamp. Its light reaches the Caravanserai’s courtyard, and a toast is raised to the tower.
47. **One More Night.** Sixteen messages keyed. Under the logbook’s last entry, you write tonight’s date in your own hand.
48. **Lamp 1.** Thirty-seven lamps. In the logbook’s last entry, a single number: one more night.
49. **Lamp D.** Lamp thirty-eight. The lens turns a full circle for the first time in forty winters.
50. **Lamp 6.** The thirty-ninth lamp. The Guildmaster brings two cups of tea and sits in the empty chair beside you.
51. **Lamp 0.** Forty lamps, the zero with the long, slow sound. The whole stair burns now, from the gate to the lantern room.
52. **Lamp X.** The last lamp. Every sound the tower knows is yours. The beacon waits for its answer.
53. **Trial: The Beacon’s Answer.** The beacon blazes. Its light sweeps the water and the square, and from the radio room comes a clear, steady signal: the far voice, answering at last. The radio room door swings open. Inside, a chair, a key and a headset wait for an operator ready to hold a conversation.
54. **Rank: Listener.** The Guildmaster hangs a pair of ear cups on your peg. “Listener. You hear what the tower hears.”

### Chapter 6: A Hand for the Key

The radio room stands open, and the far voice is clear in the headset: a callsign, a question mark, a pause. It is waiting for an answer. The Guildmaster stands in the doorway and doesn’t come in. “You can hear it. So could I. Hearing was never the hard part.” She nods at the brass key. “Show me your hand first.”

55. **Twenty Messages.** Twenty messages keyed. Your hand has stopped thinking about it.
56. **Fifteen Words a Minute.** Fifteen words a minute, and clean. The relay below sounds like rain on a tin roof.
57. **Trial: The Lamp Signal.** Every letter of the message leaves your hand clean and on time. The Guildmaster, who has been counting under her breath, stops counting.
58. **Rank: Keyer.** The Guildmaster sets the brass key in your palm and closes your fingers over it. “Keyer. It was mine once. It’s better off working.”

From the lantern room the lamp spells your signal across the water, and the square below reads it letter by letter.

### Chapter 7: The Far Station

Every night at nine minutes past, the far voice calls. An answer on the air has its manners: who is calling, who is answering, when a thought ends, when the talk is over. The codebook keeps four signs for that. “Learn them,” says the Guildmaster, “and then answer it. Somebody should.”

59. **The Four Signs.** Four signs: a message ends, a contact ends, a breath, an invitation. Now you can hold a door open on the air, and close it.
60. **Trial: The Far Station.** The far voice gives its report, its name and its town as if no time had passed at all. You send your thanks and 73. It signs off, and for the first time in forty winters the logbook has a finished entry.
61. **Rank: Operator.** The Guildmaster reads your entry twice, then holds the coat out by its shoulders. “Operator. It asked for the tower and it got you. That’ll do.”
62. **A Call of Your Own.** Your own call goes up on the wall, under a row of calls nobody has used in years.
63. **First on the Air.** A stranger answers you, gives you a report and a name, and wishes you 73. The band is full of people. It always was.

The Guildmaster stayed in the doorway through the whole contact. When the far voice signed off she said, mostly to herself, “It still sends the old way.”

### Chapter 8: The Night Watch

Operators on the night watch don’t write. They listen with their hands in their pockets and know what was said. The Guildmaster stood the watch once. “I wrote everything down, that last night,” she says. “Every letter. I was so busy writing that I never answered.”

64. **Words by Ear.** The words have stopped arriving as letters. They arrive whole, like faces.
65. **Trial: The Night Watch.** Twenty words go by at full speed and you write none of them down. When the last one fades, you say them back to the empty room, in order.
66. **Rank: Telegraphist.** She lights the night lantern and hands it to you. “Telegraphist. The watch is yours. I’m going to sleep through one, for once.”

You stand the watch with your hands in your pockets. At nine minutes past, the far voice sends its two letters, and this time you know them without thinking. They are the Guildmaster’s initials.

### Chapter 9: On the Air

The band is busy at night. Operators on three coasts have heard that the tower is back, and each wants it in their log. The far voice is among them, patient, and faster than the rest. To hold a contact at its speed, you’ll have to keep up with everyone else first.

67. **Three in the Log.** Three contacts. A new station has heard about you and starts calling.
68. **Six in the Log.** Six contacts. An operator who sends like a metronome puts out a call for the tower by name.
69. **Ten in the Log.** Ten contacts. Someone comes on at twenty-five words a minute and doesn’t slow down for anybody.
70. **No Repeats.** Twenty-five words a minute from start to finish, and you asked for nothing twice. Before signing off, the other station adds two letters: FB. Fine business.
71. **Rank: Signal Master.** The Guildmaster sets the cap on your head herself. “Signal Master. There were four of us when I was young. Now there’s you.”

That night the far voice changes its call. It no longer asks for the tower. It asks for you.

### Chapter 10: Ghost of the Ether

There is a speed past which code stops being sound and becomes meaning, the way a sentence in your own language does. The old operators called anyone who worked there a ghost: on the air they were only ever a rhythm, never seen. The far voice works at that speed when it thinks nobody is listening. The Guildmaster knows, because she used to work there with it.

72. **Thirty by Ear.** A callsign goes by at thirty words a minute, and you have it before it ends.
73. **Thirty by Hand.** Thirty words a minute off your own key. The relay below can’t keep up; it hums instead.
74. **Rank: Ghost of the Ether.** No ceremony. One evening the cloak is on your peg, gray as static, with a note in the Guildmaster’s hand: “Ghost of the Ether. Don’t let it go to your head. Nobody can see you.” The far voice is still calling. One night soon, it won’t be for you.

## The Long Road In

274 steps in 114 chapters.

### Chapter 1: The Stranger at the Gate

Dust on your boots, the sun going down behind you. The caravanserai’s gate stands open, and lamplight spills across the courtyard. Over the arch, words are painted that you can’t read. The innkeeper, a broad woman with flour on her sleeves, looks you over. “You can’t read the signs on the road? Then sit. Every traveler starts with a few letters.”

1. **A Few Letters.**
2. **At the Door.** The innkeeper pours a cup you didn’t ask for. “Before letters, manners. Nobody walks past a door here without a word.” You say it back, badly. She pours anyway.
3. **The Slate.** The scribe wipes the slate with his sleeve and hands you the chalk. Your ب has its dot in the wrong place. He moves it with one finger and says nothing.
4. **Milestone 1.** The innkeeper taps the table: بيت. “House. You’re in one. Now you can say so.” The first milestone on the road catches the light.
5. **The Fountain.** The children by the fountain shout letters at each other like a game. You catch one, then another. They start shouting them at you.
6. **Milestone 2.** A boy runs past with a sack of rice. رز, he shouts, and grins when you read it off the sack.
7. **Please and Thanks.** The cook hands you a plate, and this time you have the word for it. She pretends to be shocked.
8. **The Storyteller.** The storyteller holds up a card with one short word on it and waits. He has all evening. You read it. He holds up another.
9. **Milestone 3.** An old man by the fountain taps his wrist. “دم,” he says: blood. “Family is written there first.”
10. **A Line of Letters.** A whole line across the slate, with no help. The scribe turns it around to show the boy with the rice, who is not impressed.
11. **Milestone 4.** Two merchants argue over شاي. You read the word on the tin before they do. One of them laughs and pours you a glass.
12. **Counting.** The innkeeper counts the cups on the tray out loud, slowly, for your benefit. You count them back.
13. **Milestone 5.** The kitchen door opens: جاج on the board tonight. The cook calls something from deep in the throat, a ح that no Hebrew letter quite holds. You try it. She nods: almost.
14. **The Echo Well.** The cook leans over the well and calls a word down it. It comes back twice, once from the throat and once from the chest. Now you can tell which is which.
15. **Milestone 6.** A traveler from the south says ق like a g, a man from the hills like a k, the innkeeper not at all. “Three ways, one letter,” she shrugs. “Listen to who’s talking.”
16. **A Catch in the Throat.** ع is the letter that makes the well ring. You have stopped hearing it as a pause and started hearing it as a letter.
17. **Two Lines of Letters.** Two lines now. The scribe has started saving you the good chalk.
18. **Milestone 7.** The heavy letters. The storyteller makes them sound like a door closing. A merchant counts his coins: صفر, zero. A pilgrim sighs: سفر, the road. One heavy letter apart, and now you hear it.
19. **Two Rough Sounds.** One of them scrapes and one of them breathes. The old man by the fountain says both for you until you stop guessing.
20. **Milestone 8.** The last milestone. The innkeeper takes down the painted sign over the arch and hands it to you. “Read it.”
21. **The Whole Slate.** Every letter of the script, from memory, in your own hand. The scribe doesn’t wipe it off. He leans the slate against the wall where people can see it.
22. **Deeper Still.** The last pair before the gate. The well gives them back one after the other, and you name each before the echo fades.
23. **Trial: The Painted Sign.** You read it aloud, every word. The innkeeper writes your name in her book, in Arabic, and turns it so you can read that too. “ضيف,” she says. “Guest. Your cup is filled first now.”
24. **Rank: Guest.** The house sash goes over your shoulder. ضيف: guest. People move over on the bench without being asked.

### Chapter 2: The Carter’s Greeting

A cart rattles in through the gate, and its driver jumps down before it stops. Salim greets the innkeeper, the cook, the boy with the rice and then you, twice each. “مرحبا! Hello! You’re the new guest? Sit with me. On the road, a greeting opens more doors than a key.”

25. **The Innkeeper’s Table.** The innkeeper clears a place at her own table. “Letters are for signs,” she says. “Words are for people. Sit.”
26. **Sit with Salim.** Salim asks where you’re from and waits for the answer, however long it takes. It takes a while. He has time, and tea.
27. **The Carter’s Greeting.** Salim stands and greets a stranger at the gate, then waves you over to do the next one yourself. You do.
28. **Trial: The Carter’s Roll Call.** Salim claps you on the back. “You greet like you were born on this road.” From now on, his cup sits next to yours at the innkeeper’s table.

### Chapter 3: The Grandmother’s Questions

An old woman in an embroidered dress takes the cushion across from you and studies you over her glasses. “Who are you? Where are you from? Who are your people?” This is Umm Khalil, and she won’t stop asking until she knows.

29. **Questions.** Umm Khalil’s questions come faster than you can answer them. At the coffee corner you learn to ask a few of your own.
30. **The Grandmother’s Questions.** A traveler asks where the well is. Before Umm Khalil can answer, you do.
31. **Trial: Umm Khalil’s Questions.** “Now I know who you are,” Umm Khalil says, and pats your hand. Her cushion stays across from yours at the table.

### Chapter 4: The Clockmender’s Count

A thin man spreads a cloth on the table and lays out tiny gears in rows. Yusuf mends clocks, and he counts everything: steps, coins, minutes until supper. “One, two, three. Count with me, or you’ll lose your place.”

32. **At the Shop.** Yusuf needs lamp oil before the shop closes. He sends you, with the right words and the exact coins.
33. **The Heavy S.** Yusuf taps two gears together. One rings and one thuds. “ص,” he says, of the thud.
34. **The Clockmender’s Count.** Yusuf plans his week out loud, day by day, and lets you finish the list.
35. **Trial: The Clockmender’s Count.** Every clock Yusuf mended chimes at once. “You kept time,” he says. His cloth of gears stays on the corner of the table.

### Chapter 5: The Baker’s Daughter

The smell reaches the table before she does: Rana, the baker’s daughter, with a basket of warm bread on her hip. “You’ve been eating here for weeks and you still point at things. Tonight you ask for them.”

36. **Getting to Know You.** Rana wants to know everything about you before the bread is out of the oven.
37. **The Heavy D.** Rana drops a full sack of flour and then an empty one. “Hear it? ض is the full one.”
38. **The Baker’s Daughter.** You order a whole supper, from soup to tea, and Rana writes it down without a single point.
39. **Trial: Rana’s Bread Basket.** Rana tears the last loaf in two and gives you half. “You eat like family now.” Her basket has a place at the table.

### Chapter 6: The Mason’s Plan

A big man with plaster dust in his beard unrolls a drawing across the table: a house, room by room. Abu Fadi is building it for his son. “Help me with the words, guest. A house is nothing but its rooms.”

40. **At the Coffee Corner.** Abu Fadi takes his coffee without sugar and expects you to order it right.
41. **The Heavy T.** Abu Fadi knocks on plaster, then on stone. “ط,” he says, with his knuckles on the stone.
42. **The Mason’s Plan.** Abu Fadi shows you where the furniture will stand, and you say where each piece goes.
43. **Trial: The Mason’s Plan.** Abu Fadi rolls up the plan and writes your name on the corner. “Every house needs a guest.” His plans stay on the table.

### Chapter 7: The Guide at the Gate

A wiry man in a dusty kufiya sits down with a map and a cup of coffee. Nasser leads travelers through the hills and the towns beyond. “You can’t stay in the courtyard forever. Out there, you need to ask the way.”

44. **Which Way.** Nasser won’t point. He makes you ask.
45. **Three Ways to Say It.** Nasser says ق three ways in one sentence, to show off. You catch all three.
46. **The Guide at the Gate.** Nasser plans a whole journey with you: the bus, the road, the station at the end.
47. **Trial: The Guide’s Map.** Nasser folds the map and tucks it into your bag. “Now you can find your way back.” His coffee cup stays on the table.

### Chapter 8: The Painter’s Portrait

A young woman sets up a small easel by the lamp and squints at you. Leila paints portraits for travelers to send home. “Sit still. And tell me what I’m painting: your eyes, your hair, the color of your coat.”

48. **Today and Tomorrow.** Leila will finish the portrait tomorrow, she says. Or the day after. You learn the words for both.
49. **The Painter’s Portrait.** You describe the innkeeper to Leila, and she paints her from your words alone.
50. **Trial: Leila’s Portrait.** Leila turns the easel around: it’s you, at the innkeeper’s table, mid-word. She hangs it over the table, where it stays.

### Chapter 9: The Spice Seller’s Scales

A man with a brass scale and a dozen little sacks takes the end of the table. Hassan sells spices, and he never gives a price without a smile and a story. “You want za‘tar? First you bargain.”

51. **In Hassan’s Voice.** Hassan says a whole sentence and has you say it along with him, on top of him, the way he learned to sell. By the fifth one, the well can’t tell you apart.
52. **The Spice Seller’s Scales.** You buy for the whole inn: spices, coffee, sugar, and you pay what it’s worth.
53. **Trial: The Spice Seller’s Scales.** Hassan weighs out a little bag of za‘tar and won’t take your coins. “For a friend.” His scale stays at the end of the table.

### Chapter 10: The Teacher’s Lesson

A woman with a stack of exercise books sits down to mark them by the lamp. Miss Huda teaches the village children, and she asks about your work, your days and your plans. “Everyone here does something. What do you do?”

54. **The Teacher’s Lesson.** You describe your whole working day to Miss Huda, from waking up to closing the door.
55. **Trial: Miss Huda’s Lesson.** Miss Huda writes “excellent” on your page in red ink, in Arabic. Her stack of books stays on the table.

### Chapter 11: The Poet’s Evening

An old man with a walking stick takes the cushion nearest the lamp. Jamil was a shepherd, and now he makes poems about the weather and the heart. “Tell me how the day was. Not the work: the sky, and how you felt.”

56. **The Poet’s Evening.** You tell Jamil about a happy day and a sad one, and he listens to both.
57. **Trial: The Poet’s Evening.** Jamil closes his eyes and nods. “You speak from the heart now.” The whole table is full: every regular in their place, and you among them.
58. **Rank: Traveler.** The innkeeper ties the keffiyeh herself. مسافر: traveler. “You could leave tomorrow and get by,” she says. “Don’t.”

### Chapter 12: The Tailor’s Fitting

Just when the table seemed full, the gate opens again: a caravan from the coast, its people dusty and loud. First down is Samira, a tailor with pins in her collar and a tape measure around her neck. She looks at your shirt and sighs. “Who sewed this? Sit. Tell me what you wear, and in what colors.”

59. **Three Lines.** You say three things to Samira in a row, and she answers each one as if it were ordinary. It is.
60. **Rank: Neighbor.** A sprig of olive for your collar. جار: neighbor. The night watchman stops asking who you are.
61. **The Tailor’s Fitting.** You tell Samira your shirt is dirty, and she laughs. “Clean, then. Wash it, and I’ll mend the sleeve.”
62. **Trial: Samira’s Fitting.** Samira pins a new collar on your old shirt. “Now you look like you live here.” She keeps a chair for you by her sewing basket.

### Chapter 13: The Grandfather’s Family

An old man arrives with what seems like half the caravan behind him: sons, daughters, cousins and a baby asleep on someone’s shoulder. Abu Sami sits, and they all find a place around him. “My family,” he says. “Learn their names, and how each one is mine.”

63. **The Grandfather’s Family.** You tell Abu Sami about your own family and your friends at home. He listens, and remembers them all.
64. **Trial: Abu Sami’s Family Tree.** Abu Sami puts the baby in your arms for a moment. “There. Now you’re family too.” His grandchildren wave to you every morning.

### Chapter 14: The Apprentice’s Kitchen

A thin boy with a sack of onions follows the caravan’s cook into the kitchen. Karim is learning to cook, and he talks about food the whole time he chops. “Breakfast, lunch, dinner. Tell me what you want, and I’ll bring it.”

65. **The Apprentice’s Kitchen.** You tell Karim his soup is tasty, then that it’s a little sour. He tastes it and agrees.
66. **Trial: Karim’s Menu.** Karim writes your name on the kitchen wall, next to the cook’s. “Our best customer.” He saves you the last piece of cake.

### Chapter 15: The New House

A young woman with a key on a string sits down, tired and happy. Mona has just rented a house at the edge of the village, and it’s empty. “I need a lamp, a mirror, pillows, everything. Help me make a list?”

67. **The New House.** You help Mona make tea in her new kitchen, then close the windows and turn off the lights.
68. **Trial: Mona’s Housewarming.** Mona gives you a spare key on its own string. “Come for coffee whenever you like.” The house has a picture on the wall now, and it’s yours.

### Chapter 16: The Student’s Notebook

A young man in glasses sits with a pile of books and doesn’t look up. Tariq is home from college and has a test in a week. “English, French, a little German. And Arabic grammar, which is the hardest. Will you study with me?”

69. **The Student’s Notebook.** You answer all of Tariq’s homework questions out loud, and he checks them against the book.
70. **Trial: Tariq’s Exam.** Tariq passes his test, and says it’s half yours. He gives you his old dictionary, with your name inside the cover.

### Chapter 17: The Post Rider

A woman on a bicycle rides straight through the gate with a bag of letters. Dalia carries the mail between the villages and she knows everyone’s news. “Letters, newspapers, and now phones that never stop. Help me sort this, will you?”

71. **The Post Rider.** You read Dalia the newspaper’s headlines while she fixes her bicycle.
72. **Trial: Dalia’s Mailbag.** Dalia gives you the first letter ever addressed to you here, care of the innkeeper. Inside: a card from Salim, with a drawing of his cart.

### Chapter 18: The Musician’s Night

When the lamps are lit, a man with an oud on his back sits near the fire. Fares plays at weddings in every town on the road. “Music, dancing, games: that’s what a free evening is for. What do you do for fun?”

73. **The Musician’s Night.** Fares stops in the middle of a song and you sing the next line. The table cheers.
74. **Trial: Fares’s Song.** Fares writes a verse about the traveler who learned to sing in Arabic. He plays it every time you walk in.

### Chapter 19: The Road to Amman

A woman in a driver’s cap comes in, shaking dust from her coat. Amal drives the bus from the border to Amman and back, and she has seen every road between. “Where are you going next? Tell me, and I’ll tell you the way.”

75. **The Road to Amman.** Amal asks about your journey here, from the day you set off. You tell it to her in Arabic.
76. **Trial: Amal’s Timetable.** Amal gives you a ticket with no date on it. “For when you’re ready to see more.” It goes in your pocket, next to Mona’s key.

### Chapter 20: The Shepherd’s Hillside

A boy comes in at dusk smelling of goats and grass. Ziad keeps his family’s flock on the hills above the village, and he knows every animal by name. “Come up with me tomorrow. I’ll show you the mountain, the lake and all my goats.”

77. **The Shepherd’s Hillside.** At night, Ziad shows you the moon and the stars over the desert, and you name them with him.
78. **Trial: Ziad’s Flock.** Ziad gives you a little bell from his best goat. “So the hills know you.” You hear it ring every time you walk in the courtyard.

### Chapter 21: The Merchant’s Ledger

The caravan’s owner comes in last, with a heavy book under his arm. Abu Nabil counts everything: sacks, coins, days, miles. “A merchant who can’t count in Arabic gets cheated twice a week. Sit, and check my sums.”

79. **The Merchant’s Ledger.** You add up a whole page of Abu Nabil’s ledger, and you find the mistake he left in to test you.
80. **Trial: Abu Nabil’s Accounts.** Abu Nabil closes the ledger. “You count better than my sons.” He writes your name in it, with a line all to yourself.

### Chapter 22: The Traveler from Baghdad

A second caravan comes in from the east, bigger than the first, with tents rolled on the camels’ backs. Khalid has crossed every Arab country selling carpets, and he has a cousin in each. “Syrian, Iraqi, Yemeni, Moroccan: every one of them speaks Arabic, and none of them speaks it the same. Where are you from?”

81. **The Traveler from Baghdad.** You tell Khalid where each of his carpets was made, from the pattern and his hints.
82. **Trial: Khalid’s Carpets.** Khalid gives you a small carpet from Iraq. “For your room. So you can say you’ve been somewhere.”

### Chapter 23: The Sailor’s Stories

A man with a sea-captain’s beard and a sailor’s rolling walk takes a seat. Elias has sailed from Haifa to every port in Europe, and he tells it all at once. “France, Spain, Italy, Greece. I’ve been everywhere the sea goes. Ask me anything.”

83. **The Sailor’s Stories.** You tell Elias where you’d sail first. He nods: a good choice, though cold in winter.
84. **Trial: The Captain’s Map.** Elias gives you a brass compass. “So you always know which way home is, and which way the sea is.”

### Chapter 24: The Far Side of the Map

A woman with three cameras around her neck sits down and orders tea in careful Arabic. Mei is from Taiwan, and she has photographed markets in forty countries. “I learned Arabic the way you are learning it. Look at my pictures, and tell me where they are.”

85. **The Far Side of the Map.** Mei asks which continent you’d visit first. You tell her, and why, in Arabic.
86. **Trial: Mei’s Album.** Mei takes your picture at the innkeeper’s table, with every regular in it. She sends a copy with Dalia’s next mail.

### Chapter 25: The Surveyor’s Chain

A man in a pressed shirt comes in with a long measuring chain and a book of numbers. Rami measures the new road the caravans will use. “Kilometers, kilograms, thousands of stones. And the work must be done before September.”

87. **The Surveyor’s Chain.** You check Rami’s count of the stones for the road: five thousand, three hundred. He was right.
88. **Trial: The Surveyor’s Count.** Rami names a milestone on the new road after the traveler who checked his numbers. It’s your name, carved in Arabic.

### Chapter 26: The Market Stall

A woman with a cart of clothes sets up beside the gate and comes in for her lunch. Nour sells shirts, jeans and coats in the market, and she never lets a customer pay too much. “Small, medium, large. Cash or card. Come and try it, and I’ll tell you the price.”

89. **The Market Stall.** You ask the waiter for the bill for Nour’s lunch and yours, and pay it before she can.
90. **Trial: Nour’s Bargain.** Nour gives you a belt from her cart. “No charge. You brought me three customers today.”

### Chapter 27: The Doctor’s Rounds

A doctor with a leather bag comes in from the village late at night. Nadia visits the sick in all the villages around, and she eats whatever the innkeeper puts in front of her. “Eat well, sleep well, and you won’t need me. Now, who here is ill?”

91. **The Doctor’s Rounds.** You help Nadia at the hospital for a morning, and tell her what each patient says.
92. **Trial: The Doctor’s Questions.** Nadia writes you a prescription: “Rest one day a week.” The innkeeper pins it on the wall.

### Chapter 28: The Holiday Crowd

A big man in a bright shirt comes in with a guitar and a phone full of pictures. Antoine went to Brazil forty years ago and has come home for a holiday. “Music, dancing, swimming, and a selfie with everyone! A day out isn’t a day out without it.”

93. **The Holiday Crowd.** Antoine takes a selfie with you at the pool, and you tell him to send it to everyone.
94. **Trial: Antoine’s Day Out.** Antoine leaves you his guitar when he flies home. “Play for Fares. Tell him the song is from Brazil.”

### Chapter 29: The Old Friend’s Words

The first regular you met comes back through the gate with his cart. Salim has been on the road for a season, and he talks faster than ever. “You know the words. Now learn the other words: the ones people use when they’re not being careful.”

95. **The Old Friend’s Words.** You tell Salim what you think about the road, and he says you sound like a local.
96. **Trial: Salim’s Return.** Salim laughs. “Now you even argue like us.” He sits back in his old seat, next to yours.

### Chapter 30: The Barber’s Chair

A barber sets up his chair under the arches every Friday, and today he comes in for tea. Abu Hani shaves the whole village and hears everyone’s news. “A beard, a mustache, a cut. Sit, and tell me about your week, from waking up to falling asleep.”

97. **The Barber’s Chair.** Abu Hani shaves you, and you describe your whole day to him without stopping.
98. **Trial: The Barber’s Gossip.** Abu Hani gives you a free shave every Friday from now on. “A regular’s right.”

### Chapter 31: The Spice Road

The second caravan’s cook comes into the kitchen and argues with the innkeeper about salt. Umm Ali cooks for forty travelers a day, and she tastes everything. “Salty, sweet, bitter, spicy. A cook needs a tongue first and hands second.”

99. **The Spice Road.** Umm Ali asks how to cook the falafel: fried or baked. You tell her, and she agrees.
100. **Trial: Umm Ali’s Kitchen.** Umm Ali teaches you her falafel. You make it for the whole table, and nobody leaves any.

### Chapter 32: The Builder’s House

Abu Fadi the mason comes back, and his son is with him. They’re building a house in the village with a basement, three floors and an elevator, the first in the valley. “Come and see it. Tell me what goes in each room.”

101. **The Builder’s House.** Abu Fadi asks where you’d put the curtains and the carpet. You tell him, and he writes it down.
102. **Trial: The New House’s Rooms.** Abu Fadi gives you the key to the roof. “The best view in the valley. Come up whenever you like.”

### Chapter 33: The Hiring Fair

A woman with a list sits down and starts asking everyone what they do. Wafa runs the new factory in town and needs workers. “Engineers, mechanics, a nurse, a cook. And someone who can talk to all of them.”

103. **The Hiring Fair.** Wafa holds a meeting with her new workers, and you explain each job in Arabic.
104. **Trial: Wafa’s Interviews.** Wafa offers you a job. You say no, kindly, in Arabic. She laughs and says the offer stays open.

### Chapter 34: The Long Way Round

Amal the bus driver comes back with news: the new road is open, and she drives to the coast and the border now. “Bus, ship, plane: I’ve booked them all for travelers. Now you can go abroad and back, if you know how to ask.”

105. **The Long Way Round.** Amal tells you how to check in at the airport. You tell it back to her, step by step.
106. **Trial: Amal’s New Route.** Amal stamps the ticket she gave you long ago. It’s good for anywhere now, whenever you’re ready.

### Chapter 35: The Innkeeper’s Story

On the quietest night of the year, the innkeeper sits down at her own table for the first time. She pours two cups, one for you. “Everyone has told you their story. Mine is short. Do you want to hear it?”

107. **The Innkeeper’s Story.** She asks what you’ll do one day, when you leave. You tell her your plan, and she says it’s a good one.
108. **Trial: The Innkeeper’s Night.** The innkeeper turns her book to the first page. Under “guest,” she has written a new word next to your name: صاحب. Friend of the house.
109. **A Thousand Words.** A thousand words. You stopped counting some time ago. The innkeeper didn’t.
110. **Twenty Evenings.** Twenty evenings of talk at this table. Somebody has started saving your seat.
111. **Rank: Friend.** The innkeeper sets a small brass coffee pot at your place. صاحب: friend. You pour for the others now.

### Chapter 36: The Wedding Letter

A letter comes for the innkeeper, and she reads it twice and laughs out loud. Her niece Hana is getting married, here, at the inn, and the whole family is coming. Hana arrives first, a week early, to help.

112. **The Wedding Letter.** She tells you that her grandmother was born in this house, long ago, and that the family has come back for every birth and every wedding since.
113. **Trial: Hana’s Guest List.** Hana writes your name on the guest list, between her grandmother and the twins. “You’re family now,” she says. “Family comes early to a wedding.”

### Chapter 37: The Fitting

Samira the tailor comes back through the gate with a trunk of cloth and a mouth full of pins. The bride needs a dress, the groom needs a suit, and the whole family wants something new for the wedding.

114. **The Fitting.** Hana’s grandmother opens her own box of jewelry: a gold bracelet, silver earrings, an old necklace. You hold each one up to the light.
115. **Trial: The Wedding Clothes.** Samira pins a silk flower to your collar. “For the wedding,” she says. “Don’t lose it in your pocket.”

### Chapter 38: The Grandfather’s Aches

The bride’s grandfather arrives on his son’s arm, holding his back. Yousef is eighty and proud of it, and he’ll tell anyone who listens about every one of his pains.

116. **The Grandfather’s Aches.** By the morning he feels better. He tells you his health is fine and his weight is perfect, and he asks for a second breakfast.
117. **Trial: Yousef’s Aches.** Yousef pats your hand. “You listen like a good doctor,” he says. “And you don’t give me any medicine. That’s the best kind.”

### Chapter 39: The Pastry Table

A woman comes in with flour up to her elbows and a cart of trays. Lina bakes for every wedding from here to the coast, and she has three days to fill the inn’s longest table.

118. **The Pastry Table.** She shows you the sauces for the feast, and you help her roast the chickens. Nobody goes hungry at a wedding.
119. **Trial: Lina’s Trays.** Lina gives you the first cookie from the last tray. “Baked today,” she says. “Don’t tell the guests.”

### Chapter 40: The Harvest Cart

A farmer drives his cart through the gate, piled high with fruit and vegetables for the feast. Abu Saleh grows wheat and corn on the hills and keeps bees beside his palm trees.

120. **The Harvest Cart.** He tells you about his farm, his fields and his bees. He plants a seed in a flowerpot for the bride, a gift that will grow.
121. **Trial: Abu Saleh’s Cart.** Abu Saleh puts a branch of his palm tree over the inn’s door. “For luck,” he says. “Weddings need it.”

### Chapter 41: The Big Clean

The inn has to shine before the guests arrive, and the innkeeper hires a handyman. Tariq mends fences, fixes sinks and can build anything out of wood, glass or metal.

122. **The Big Clean.** By evening the inn is quiet and bright. He lights a candle in every window and asks you if it’s ready. It is.
123. **Trial: Tariq’s Chores.** Tariq hands you his hammer. “Next time,” he says, “you fix the fence.”

### Chapter 42: The Schoolteacher

The groom’s aunt arrives with a bag of books. Huda has taught at the village school for thirty years, from kindergarten to high school, and she can’t stop teaching even at a wedding.

124. **The Schoolteacher.** She asks about your level and your grammar. She tests your vocabulary and your pronunciation, and she gives you a good grade.
125. **Trial: Huda’s Lesson.** Huda writes a sentence on the blackboard and signs it: “A very good student.” She doesn’t erase it.

### Chapter 43: The Wedding Pictures

Mei the photographer comes back for the wedding, with her cameras and a laptop this time. She’ll take the pictures, and she needs help with the screen, the printer and the files.

126. **The Wedding Pictures.** In the evening she plays a song on the radio station and puts the news on, and everyone watches the old videos of the family on her screen.
127. **Trial: Mei’s Slideshow.** Mei prints one picture for you: you and the innkeeper, laughing at the table. “Keep it,” she says. “It’s the best one.”

### Chapter 44: The Long Night

On the wedding night the band arrives: a drummer, an oud player and Fares, who sings and never sits down. There’s music, dancing and games until morning.

128. **The Long Night.** The young men play soccer in the courtyard, and Fares scores a goal with his foot and his head. His team wins, and he takes a picture of everyone.
129. **Trial: The Wedding Night.** Fares hands you the drum for the last song. “Everyone plays at a wedding,” he says. “Even the guests.”

### Chapter 45: The Road Away

In the morning the newlyweds leave, and the whole inn comes out to the gate. Hana and her groom are going on a trip: to the coast, the islands and the hills on the other side of the valley.

130. **The Road Away.** They load the car, and Hana gives you a helmet for the motorcycle she leaves behind. She says the waves and the sand can wait for you too.
131. **Trial: The Newlyweds’ Route.** The car goes out through the gate and down into the valley. The innkeeper turns her book to a new page and writes the date at the top. “Another year,” she says. “Who comes next?”

### Chapter 46: The Small Words

A new year at the inn begins with Nasser, an old storyteller who sits by the fire every evening. His stories are long, but he says the small words carry them: now, before, after, still.

132. **The Small Words.** Nasser says a good storyteller is careful: he never lies, he only chooses. You choose the ending together.
133. **Trial: Nasser’s Story.** Nasser puts another log on the fire. “Enough for tonight,” he says. “But a story is never really finished.”

### Chapter 47: Change for a Hundred

Rami sets up a little counter by the gate, where travelers change money and sign for their rooms. He’s never without a pen, a ruler and a roll of tape.

134. **Change for a Hundred.** A thief steals a purse at the gate, but the chef and the hairdresser catch him at the door. Rami asks you to sign as a witness.
135. **Trial: Rami’s Ledger.** Rami counts the day’s coins and gives you one for luck. “Save it,” he says. “That’s how I started.”

### Chapter 48: Fog on the Hills

Yasmin climbs to the inn’s roof every morning to read the sky. Travelers ask her before they go: is there fog, heat or a storm coming?

136. **Fog on the Hills.** The sun comes out, the ice melts, and Yasmin says the road is clear. You measure the distance with her: three miles to the river.
137. **Trial: Yasmin’s Forecast.** Yasmin folds her map. “The weather always changes,” she says. “That’s why I’m never out of work.”

### Chapter 49: Count Them Twice

Master Karim teaches the village children in the inn’s back room. Mornings are sums; afternoons are writing: verbs, plurals, commas and periods.

138. **Count Them Twice.** The beginners read a paragraph out loud. You help them find the verb and the plural, and put the periods where they go.
139. **Trial: Karim’s Sums.** Karim writes a million on the board, with all its zeros. “Count them twice,” he says. “Then count them again.”

### Chapter 50: Every Tongue

Elena comes from far away and speaks five languages already: Greek, Portuguese, a little Korean, some Farsi and some Hebrew. Now she wants Arabic, and she wants to see the towns of the land.

140. **Every Tongue.** She practices listening and spelling with you each night, and says she hopes to come back speaking better than you.
141. **Trial: Elena’s Map.** Elena writes a word in her notebook in six alphabets. “Foreign today,” she says, “but not for long.”

### Chapter 51: The Vet’s Rounds

Dr. Rana stops at the inn on her rounds through the villages. Her bag is full of medicine, and her car is full of animals: a parrot in a cage, a turtle in a box, and a dog with a red collar.

142. **The Vet’s Rounds.** In the evening Rana tells stories about bears, tigers, whales and sharks. The children ask which is the strongest.
143. **Trial: Rana’s Rounds.** Rana lets the butterfly out of the window. “Not every patient needs medicine,” she says. “Some just need the door open.”

### Chapter 52: The Neighbors’ Feast

The holidays come close together this year, and the two neighbors across the lane decide to cook for both. Mariam goes to church, Um Khaled hears the call to prayer from her window, and the whole street eats at one table.

144. **The Neighbors’ Feast.** Um Khaled says that in the eighties the street was smaller and everyone knew everyone. These days, she says, it takes a feast.
145. **Trial: The Neighbors’ Table.** Mariam and Um Khaled wash the dishes together, side by side. “Next year at my house,” says one. “No,” says the other, “at mine.”

### Chapter 53: The Night Guard’s Report

Sami guards the inn at night, and he has seen everything: smokers on the roof, a drunk singer in the courtyard, and a man who tried to smuggle cigarettes in a guitar case.

146. **The Night Guard’s Report.** Sami doesn’t arrest him. He writes his report, slowly, and asks you to read it out loud to check the spelling.
147. **Trial: Sami’s Report.** Sami turns off his lamp as the sun comes up. “Another quiet night,” he says, and winks. “Mostly.”

### Chapter 54: Hands for the East Wing

Winter comes, and the innkeeper decides to rebuild the old east wing at last. Bassam the foreman sets up a table in the courtyard to hire hands, with a list of every trade he needs.

148. **Hands for the East Wing.** Old Abu Mahmoud says he retired years ago, but his pension is small and he misses his trade. Bassam makes him the technician who checks everyone’s work.
149. **Trial: The Foreman’s Hiring.** Bassam rolls up his list. “Thirty hands for thirty rooms,” he says. “Now the real work starts.”

### Chapter 55: The Clinic by the Gate

With so many workers on the site, someone always gets hurt. Samar the nurse opens a small clinic in a room by the gate: a bed, a cupboard of bandages and a long line of patients.

150. **The Clinic by the Gate.** He comes back with a diagnosis, a treatment and a box of pills, and he admits that Samar was right. You write his doses on the box in Arabic.
151. **Trial: Samar’s Clinic.** Samar locks the cupboard at sunset. “Nobody died today,” she says, and laughs. “That’s a good day in a clinic.”

### Chapter 56: Kunafeh for the Workers

Every afternoon at four, Abu Jamal comes up from Nablus with a tray of kunafeh as big as a wheel, and the work stops. He says no wall was ever built on an empty stomach.

152. **Kunafeh for the Workers.** One evening the kunafeh goes wrong and comes out bland. Abu Jamal tastes it, laughs and gives the whole tray to the children.
153. **Trial: The Sweet Maker’s Tray.** Abu Jamal wipes the empty tray. “Tomorrow, baklava,” he says. “If the walls go up faster.”

### Chapter 57: The Toolbox Upstairs

The first finished room is rented to Widad, a schoolteacher who moves in with a suitcase and a toolbox. By the second day she has fixed the shutters and hung every picture straight.

154. **The Toolbox Upstairs.** In the evenings Widad mows the little lawn behind the kitchen and digs a garden. She says a house isn’t a home until you’ve dug in it.
155. **Trial: Widad’s Toolbox.** Widad puts her toolbox under the bed. “Now,” she says, “I can finally unpack my books.”

### Chapter 58: The Long Night

Sitt Fatima is ninety, and the hammering doesn’t wake her because she doesn’t sleep. She sits up all night by the stove and asks whoever is awake to sit with her.

156. **The Long Night.** At dawn she wakes, rubs her elbow and her knees, and says her bones know the weather better than any radio. You both watch the sun come up over the new roof.
157. **Trial: Sitt Fatima’s Long Night.** Sitt Fatima pulls her blanket up. “Go to bed, habibi,” she says. “One of us has to sleep.”

### Chapter 59: The Permit

The east wing needs a permit from the town hall, and the permit needs a lawyer. Hiba arrives with a fat folder, three pens and a lot of patience.

158. **The Permit.** The council votes and the permit is signed. Hiba says the best cases are the ones that never reach the court.
159. **Trial: Hiba’s Case.** Hiba closes her folder. “All legal,” she says. “Now you can build in peace.”

### Chapter 60: The Twins Who Argue

The innkeeper’s twin grandchildren arrive for the winter holiday. Adel and Aya agree on nothing: not the weather, not the food, not the color of the new doors.

160. **The Twins Who Argue.** When Sitt Fatima falls ill, the twins stop arguing for a whole week. They take turns sitting with her, and she says she’s grateful for both.
161. **Trial: The Twins’ Argument.** Adel and Aya shake hands, for now. “We agree on one thing,” they say together. “The kunafeh.”

### Chapter 61: The Opening Night

The east wing is finished at the end of winter. Omar, the innkeeper’s eldest grandson, offers to organize the opening night, and everyone who built it is invited.

162. **The Opening Night.** Bassam, Samar, Widad and Hiba all come, and Sitt Fatima stays up late on purpose. Omar asks you to give the speech, in Arabic.
163. **Trial: Omar’s Plan.** The last guest goes home, and the new rooms are quiet. “We did it,” Omar says, and falls asleep on a chair.

### Chapter 62: Fuad’s Taxi

Spring comes, and the new east wing fills with travelers. Most of them arrive in Fuad’s old yellow taxi, which he drives as if every road were his own.

164. **Fuad’s Taxi.** A young driver overtakes on a bend and runs a red light. Fuad doesn’t shout; he puts on his seat belt and tells you about the accident he had at twenty.
165. **Trial: Fuad’s Taxi.** Fuad turns off the engine in front of the inn. “Thirty years,” he says, patting the dent in the bumper, “and she still runs.”

### Chapter 63: Tickets and Visas

Dana opens a desk in the inn’s new lobby, with maps on the wall and a stamp for every country. Half the travelers need her help, and the other half just want to talk about the sea.

166. **Tickets and Visas.** A plane is delayed, and twenty travelers wait in the lobby. Dana keeps them busy with fishing stories, and someone takes out a kite.
167. **Trial: Dana’s Tickets.** Dana stamps the last ticket. “Everyone wants to leave,” she says, “and everyone comes back to tell me about it.”

### Chapter 64: The Troupe’s Play

A traveling theater troupe takes three rooms in the east wing. Abu Rafiq, who leads them, plays the oud, and his actors rehearse in the courtyard every night.

168. **The Troupe’s Play.** On the night, the courtyard fills with spectators. The acrobat walks the rope, the audience claps, and at the intermission everyone wants coffee.
169. **Trial: The Troupe’s Play.** Abu Rafiq bows to the empty courtyard. “Every audience is different,” he says. “Tonight’s was the best.”

### Chapter 65: Majd’s Tournament

Majd coaches the town’s volleyball team and runs a small gym behind the market. When it rains, he plays chess at the inn, and he never loses.

170. **Majd’s Tournament.** The volleyball team plays against the next town and loses. Majd says a champion is someone who comes back the next week.
171. **Trial: Majd’s Tournament.** Majd folds the chessboard. “Same time next week,” he says. “And this time, protect your rook.”

### Chapter 66: The Visiting Professor

Professor Salwa comes from the university in the city to give a week of lectures at the village school. She stays in the east wing with a suitcase full of books.

172. **The Visiting Professor.** Her old teacher, now ninety, never went to school. Salwa reads her an article she wrote, and the old woman says it is too long.
173. **Trial: Salwa’s Lecture.** Salwa leaves one of her books on the innkeeper’s table. “For the next student,” she says.

### Chapter 67: The Harvest

Abu Saleh the farmer, who brought his cart to the wedding, comes back for the harvest. This time he brings his mule, his rooster and a hundred questions about the tractor he wants to buy.

174. **The Harvest.** The wheat is harvested and the grain is sold. Abu Saleh buys the tractor, and the mule looks relieved.
175. **Trial: Abu Saleh’s Harvest.** Abu Saleh drives the tractor into the courtyard to show everyone. The rooster rides on the roof.

### Chapter 68: A Business Plan

Rami the money changer has saved for years. Now he wants to start his own business, a small shop for travelers, and he asks the innkeeper to rent him the corner of the new lobby.

176. **A Business Plan.** At last the council says yes. Rami deposits his savings, wraps a present for the innkeeper, and opens his shop with fixed prices and no haggling.
177. **Trial: Rami’s Proposal.** Rami sells his first map to a traveler. He frames the coin and hangs it over the counter.
178. **Two Thousand Words.** Two thousand words. The boy with the rice, who is not a boy anymore, asks you what one of them means.
179. **A Whole Evening.** You talk a whole evening through and don’t notice until the lamp needs oil.
180. **Rank: Local.** The embroidered vest has been on someone’s needle since the week you came. ابن البلد: one of ours.

### Chapter 69: The Storm

Yasmin the weather watcher, who read the sky from the inn’s roof last year, comes back with a new compass and a bad feeling about the clouds.

181. **The Storm.** At sunrise the courtyard is a swamp, but the new east wing has held. Yasmin says the climate is changing, and she writes it all down.
182. **Trial: Yasmin’s Storm.** Yasmin folds up her charts. “Clear skies tomorrow,” she says. “I’m almost sure.”

### Chapter 70: A Summer of News

Summer comes, and with it Umm Nabil, who knows every family in three towns. She has come to find Omar a bride, and she sits at the innkeeper’s table with a notebook of names.

183. **A Summer of News.** After forty days, Umm Nabil comes back. Omar gets engaged to a widow’s daughter from the next town, and the family says Sitt Fatima would have danced.
184. **Trial: The Engagement.** Omar’s fiancée names the new rose bush in the courtyard after Sitt Fatima.

### Chapter 71: The Barber’s Mirror

Abu Hani, who cut hair before the wedding, sets up his chair again for Omar’s engagement party. Every man in town wants to look his best.

185. **The Barber’s Mirror.** A boy chokes on a candy while he waits. Abu Hani slaps his back, the candy flies out, and the whole shop claps.
186. **Trial: The Barber’s Mirror.** Abu Hani sweeps up the hair and looks at himself in the mirror. “Not bad,” he says, “for an old man.”

### Chapter 72: Buttons Before the Party

Hind runs the dry cleaner’s by the gate, and the week before the party every shirt in town comes through her door. She irons with one hand and sews buttons with the other.

187. **Buttons Before the Party.** The jeweler comes in with a brooch with a ruby and emeralds, too precious to leave anywhere else. Hind hides it in her dressing table until the party.
188. **Trial: Hind’s Ironing Board.** Hind turns off her iron at last. “Next time,” she says, “they should get engaged in winter.”

### Chapter 73: Market Day

For the party’s feast, the innkeeper sends you to market day with Tamer, who sells fish by the sea and knows every stall from the herbs to the spices.

189. **Market Day.** Back at the inn, Tamer cooks mulukhiyah, and someone brings basbousa. Nothing is stale, nothing is greasy, and nothing is left over.
190. **Trial: Tamer’s Market.** Tamer wipes his hands. “The sea gives,” he says, “and the market sells. We only cook.”

### Chapter 74: Kings and Empires

Mr. Kamal taught history at the town school for forty years. Now retired, he comes to the inn each evening and tells the children about kings, empires and revolutions.

191. **Kings and Empires.** He tells a story of a thief who broke into a palace, was sentenced, and escaped from prison through the kitchen. The children want to know if it’s true. He only smiles.
192. **Trial: Kamal’s History.** Mr. Kamal closes his old book. “History,” he says, “is just stories that really happened.”

### Chapter 75: The Reconciliation

Two families in town have quarreled for a year over a wall between their fields. Abu Jaber, an old man people trust, brings them both to the inn to make peace.

193. **The Reconciliation.** At last they reach an agreement: the wall stays, with a gate in it. They drink coffee together, and Abu Jaber says that’s how peace is made.
194. **Trial: The Peacemaker’s Coffee.** The two fathers walk home together, through the new gate. Abu Jaber finishes his coffee alone, smiling.

### Chapter 76: Two Friends

The town’s imam and its priest have been friends since school. Every Friday after prayers and every Sunday after the sermon, they meet at the innkeeper’s table to talk.

195. **Two Friends.** For Omar’s wedding, both of them come, and both bless the couple. Father Yacoub says that’s two blessings, which is never too many.
196. **Trial: The Friends’ Table.** The two friends shake hands at the gate. “Same table on Friday,” says one. “And Sunday,” says the other.

### Chapter 77: The New Clinic

Salwa, the baker’s daughter, comes home in autumn with a master’s degree and opens a small clinic in the old house by the gate. The bank financed it, and she pays it off in installments.

197. **The New Clinic.** On the clinic’s roof one night, she shows the children a comet. Her grandfather says their ancestors watched the same sky a millennium ago.
198. **Trial: Salwa’s Clinic.** Salwa hangs her degree on the wall, next to her grandmother’s embroidery. “The degree says I’m a surgeon,” she says. “The embroidery says I’m home.”

### Chapter 78: The Election

Rana writes for a newspaper in the city. She comes to the inn to cover two stories: the town’s first election for mayor in years, and a trial at the court.

199. **The Election.** Mr. Kamal tells the children how their grandparents’ town was occupied and liberated, and how the mines were cleared from the fields so the olives could grow again.
200. **Trial: Election Night.** Mr. Kamal wins, and is so absent-minded that he forgets his own speech. Rana’s story runs on the front page anyway.

### Chapter 79: The Dentist’s Chair

Two days a week, Dr. Nader brings his chair and his drill to Salwa’s clinic. The whole town suddenly remembers its teeth.

201. **The Dentist’s Chair.** Hana, the innkeeper’s niece, is in the seventh month of her pregnancy and can’t sleep for nightmares. Salwa sits with her, and the innkeeper makes sage tea.
202. **Trial: Nader’s Chair.** Dr. Nader packs his drill. “Floss,” he tells the whole inn, “and I won’t have to come back.” Nobody believes him.

### Chapter 80: Rami Quits

Rami, who once came with a business plan, is back. He has quit his job in the public sector to open an office of his own, and he wants the inn’s blessing.

203. **Rami Quits.** His mother wants him married instead. Rami says he will ask a girl’s father for her hand when the office pays for itself.
204. **Trial: Rami’s Office.** Rami turns his phone to silent for the first time in a month and falls asleep at the innkeeper’s table.

### Chapter 81: Driving Lessons

The innkeeper’s grandson wants to drive. Uncle Fawzi has taught half the town in his old manual car, and he has never lost a pupil, only a few mirrors.

205. **Driving Lessons.** That evening Uncle Fawzi smokes a hookah in the courtyard and entertains the guests with stories of his worst pupils.
206. **Trial: Fawzi’s Old Car.** The grandson passes his test on the first try. Uncle Fawzi pretends he isn’t proud, and buys him a coffee.

### Chapter 82: The Garden in Winter

Umm Yousef has kept the inn’s garden for thirty years. In winter, she says, a garden only sleeps; she still weeds it every week.

207. **The Garden in Winter.** She tells them about the year of the locusts, when she was a girl, and how the whole village beat pans to scare them away.
208. **Trial: Umm Yousef’s Garden.** Umm Yousef plants a row of poppies for spring. “Red,” she says, “for Sitt Fatima, who liked them.”

### Chapter 83: The Linguist

Dr. Ingrid has come from the north of Europe to record how people really speak. She knows standard Arabic well, and the town’s colloquial Arabic not at all.

209. **The Linguist.** By the end of her stay, her accent has changed. The innkeeper says she speaks like a woman from the market, which Ingrid takes as praise.
210. **Trial: Ingrid’s Recorder.** Dr. Ingrid leaves a copy of her notes for the inn. On the first page, in shaky Arabic: “Thank you for every word.”

### Chapter 84: Colors for the New Year

Before the new year, the innkeeper wants the old walls painted. Elias the painter comes with brushes, ladders and a hundred shades of every color.

211. **Colors for the New Year.** It pours on the last day, and the paint runs. Elias laughs, lifts his ladder and starts again.
212. **Trial: Elias’s Ladder.** The inn shines in its new colors for the new year. Elias signs his name small, in the corner, under the stairs.

### Chapter 85: Odd Jobs

After the painter comes the handyman. Abu Samir can fix anything with a screwdriver and a thimble, and he hums while he works.

213. **Odd Jobs.** The innkeeper’s grandson has bought a car with a loose pedal. Abu Samir checks the tire pressure, tightens the pedal and makes him promise to keep to the speed limit.
214. **Trial: Abu Samir’s Toolbox.** Abu Samir won’t take money from the inn. He takes a bun, a coffee and a promise to call him first next time.

### Chapter 86: A Day Out for the Class

Miss Rula brings her whole class to the inn for a spring day. She teaches grammar in the morning and lets them loose in the garden after lunch.

215. **A Day Out for the Class.** Dr. Ingrid tells them that the old gods and goddesses had names in Arabic too, and Father Yacoub tells them how the town goes down to the river at Epiphany.
216. **Trial: Rula’s Lesson.** The class goes home tired and muddy. Miss Rula leaves a drawing for the inn: a koala at the innkeeper’s table, which nobody can explain.

### Chapter 87: Two Words for Everything

Abu Nabil kept the door of the building across the square for forty years. He has a second word for everything, and he says the town’s young people only know the first.

217. **Two Words for Everything.** His wife says he has aged ten years since he stopped working. Abu Nabil says he has insomnia from all the words he still has to teach.
218. **Trial: Abu Nabil’s Door.** Abu Nabil gives the inn the old brass key to his building. “The new doors have no keys,” he says. “Someone should keep one.”

### Chapter 88: The Monk and the Swallows

When the swallows come back in spring, Brother Antoun walks down from the monastery on the hill. A porter follows him with a box of old books.

219. **The Monk and the Swallows.** A swallow builds its nest above the inn’s door. The children watch its small beak bring mud and straw all afternoon.
220. **Trial: Antoun’s Books.** Brother Antoun leaves one book at the inn, the one with the swallows drawn in its margins. He climbs back up the hill before dark.

### Chapter 89: The Sign Painter

Khalil paints the signs for half the shops in the market. He paints the letters well, but he leaves out the little marks, and the town reads them however it likes.

221. **The Sign Painter.** Khalil paints a new sign for the coffeehouse, and everyone agrees it says “coffee”. Then he paints one for the bank, and it says “account” or “the bill”, whichever you owe.
222. **Trial: Khalil’s Signs.** Khalil paints a small sign for the inn’s kitchen door. It has every mark on it, so no one can read it wrong.

### Chapter 90: The Chess Players

Every evening Abu Sami and Abu Fadi play chess at the coffeehouse by the roundabout. In their game the queen is a minister, the bishop is an elephant and the knight is a horse.

223. **The Chess Players.** When the game is over they throw dice for who pays for the tea. Abu Sami loses, and says it is a debt he will pay next week.
224. **Trial: The Last Game.** They leave their old chessboard at the inn, the one with a chipped knight. “For the next two who need an argument,” says Abu Fadi.

### Chapter 91: The Calendar Maker

Umm Fares prints the wall calendars that hang in every shop in town. This year her press jammed, and four months came out blank: January, October, November and December.

225. **The Calendar Maker.** By the day after tomorrow the new pages are dry. Umm Fares says this year’s calendar is her best, and next year’s will be better.
226. **Trial: Every Day Counted.** She hangs the first calendar of the new run behind the inn’s counter, with the innkeeper’s birthday circled.

### Chapter 92: The Evening Bus

Abu Jamil drives the evening bus to the city and back. Everyone at the stop asks him the same three things: what time, how far and how often.

227. **The Evening Bus.** He says he will stop driving one day, soon, in a bit. Everyone at the stop has heard that at least ten times.
228. **Trial: The Last Bus.** He leaves the inn a timetable written by hand. “It’s right,” he says, “at least on most days.”

### Chapter 93: Eid at the Inn

On the morning of Eid, Umm Khalil brings her whole family to the inn for breakfast: her sons, their wives and more grandchildren than anyone can count.

229. **Eid at the Inn.** A grandson apologizes for breaking a glass. Umm Khalil says nothing has changed: he was the same at the last Eid, and the one before.
230. **Trial: Eid Greetings.** They leave a tray of ka’ak and ma’amoul for the next guests. “Eid comes to everyone,” says Umm Khalil.

### Chapter 94: The Rainy Week

Hanan runs the little phone shop across the square. This week it hasn’t stopped raining, and half the town has come to her with a wet phone that won’t ring.

231. **The Rainy Week.** On the last day the sun comes out and there’s a rainbow over the square. Every phone in her shop is ringing at once, and Hanan says she has never been so proud.
232. **Trial: Every Phone Rings.** She gives the innkeeper a phone that works, for the guests to call home. “If it rains again,” she says, “you know where I am.”

### Chapter 95: The Family Reunion

Abu Samir has rented the whole inn for a family reunion. Cousins arrive from four towns and two countries, and half of them have never met.

233. **The Family Reunion.** By the evening they are all at one long table. When the plates are empty, every guest says “thank you for the meal”, and Abu Samir answers each one.
234. **Trial: Whose Son Are You?.** He writes every cousin’s name and number in the inn’s guest book, so next year’s reunion is easier to plan.

### Chapter 96: The Traveler’s Errands

Nadia has one day in town before her train, and a list of errands as long as her arm.

235. **The Traveler’s Errands.** She comes back for them just before the last bus, books a single room for one more night instead, and asks for the wifi password.
236. **Trial: One Day in Town.** She leaves a postcard for the innkeeper: “Next time I’m staying a week, at a table for two.”

### Chapter 97: The Tailor’s Fitting

Abu Rami has sewn suits in the market lane for forty years. He has never once, he says, made a jacket that didn’t fit.

237. **The Tailor’s Fitting.** It gets dark early, but the jacket is ready around seven. He wraps it in a paper bag and won’t take your bills until you’ve tried it on again.
238. **Trial: A Perfect Fit.** He sends a postcard to the inn: a drawing of the jacket, and under it, “Never a bad fit.”

### Chapter 98: The New Flat

Rasha has just rented a flat on the top floor of an apartment building across the square, and she has no idea how to keep house.

239. **The New Flat.** On Saturday she sets the table in her little dining room and invites the whole inn. She drives everyone home, the old ones in the front seat.
240. **Trial: Keeping House.** She hangs a key on the inn’s board: “For whoever needs a single bed for a night.”

### Chapter 99: The Exam Results

Wissam works part-time at the inn and studies every other night. The exam results come out on Thursday, and he can’t sit still.

241. **The Exam Results.** On Thursday he finishes work early and reads the list twice. He passed, and his team beat the other school in the same week.
242. **Trial: Passed or Failed.** He pins his results above the bar, next to a note: “Full-time from next month.”

### Chapter 100: The Tour Guide’s Trip

Ghassan takes a group down the coast every winter, and this year he has room for one more.

243. **The Tour Guide’s Trip.** On New Year’s Eve the group watches an action movie at the hotel, Ghassan plays classical music on the bus home, and someone is stung by a bee.
244. **Trial: Down the Coast.** He sends a photo of the group in front of a field of sunflowers: “Same time in five years?”
245. **Five Old Friends.** Five people at this table would notice if you missed an evening. Two of them would come looking.
246. **Rank: Storyteller.** The old storyteller takes off his cloak and puts it around your shoulders. حكواتي. “I’ve told all of mine,” he says. “Yours they haven’t heard.” The innkeeper keeps one page of her book empty. She won’t say who it’s for.

### Chapter 101: The Wedding Feast

Umm Hadi’s son is getting married, and she has taken over the inn’s kitchen for three days.

247. **The Wedding Feast.** There is minced meat, lamb, seafood and a cake with coconut and peanuts. When it’s over, she closes her eyes and takes a deep breath.
248. **Trial: Everything for the Feast.** She leaves a plate covered in a cloth for the innkeeper, and on it a note: “Eat it with your eyes closed.”

### Chapter 102: The Night Shift

Layla works the night shift at the inn’s front desk and studies psychology by day. She hasn’t slept a full night since the school year started.

249. **The Night Shift.** At dawn she hands the desk to the day clerk and heads for the lecture hall. On payday she treats the whole kitchen to breakfast.
250. **Trial: One More Exam.** She leaves her old notes on the inn’s shelf, with a line on the cover: “Passing grade: fifty. Aim for ninety.”

### Chapter 103: The Storm Chaser

Shadi drives an old pickup truck with a dashboard full of maps, and he goes where the storms go.

251. **The Storm Chaser.** At dawn he checks the spare tire, fills up at the gas pump and heads for the mountain range. If it hails, he says, you’ll hear about it.
252. **Trial: Into the Weather.** He mails the inn a photo from the cliff’s edge: a tornado far off, and his truck with one headlight on.

### Chapter 104: The Talk Show

Muna hosts the town’s evening talk show, and tonight she is broadcasting from the inn.

253. **The Talk Show.** At the end she hands out cotton candy, and asks the room what they watch at night: a thriller, a sitcom, or a fairy tale read aloud.
254. **Trial: On the Air.** She sends the inn a bookmark with the show’s name on it, and a note: “Same time next week.”

### Chapter 105: The Doctor’s Year

Dr. Rawan has looked after the town for thirty years, and she knows everyone’s migraines and everyone’s secrets.

255. **The Doctor’s Year.** Her evenings: a young couple asks when the baby is due, an old friend in a wheelchair tells jokes, and she says “may he rest in peace” for a patient she lost.
256. **Trial: A Day at the Clinic.** She leaves a box of Band-Aids at the inn’s desk, “for the next time someone falls off a stool.”

### Chapter 106: The Landlord’s Rounds

Abu Basel owns half the flats on the street, and once a month he walks his rounds with a wrench in his pocket.

257. **The Landlord’s Rounds.** He comes to the inn with dark circles under his eyes and a sore calf, sits by the fire in a borrowed bathrobe, and falls into a deep sleep.
258. **Trial: The Monthly Rounds.** He pays for his night with a new belt buckle for the innkeeper, “because the old one is held on with string.”

### Chapter 107: The Anniversary

Abu Wael and Umm Wael are in their sixties, and tonight is their fortieth wedding anniversary. Everyone is invited, even the shepherd.

259. **The Anniversary.** At the end Abu Wael writes a poem with every exclamation mark and quotation mark in place, and reads it to her with the back of his hand on his heart.
260. **Trial: Forty Years.** They leave a slice of the cake for the innkeeper and a note: “Just call us Grandma and Grandpa.”

### Chapter 108: The Deli Counter

Abu Hatem sells cheese from five countries at his counter in the market, and every morning before he opens he spends an hour at the gym.

261. **The Deli Counter.** At sunset he closes up, listens to the news of a summit and a prosecutor on the radio, and pays the mortgage on the shop with a sigh.
262. **Trial: Taste and Tell.** He sends the inn a wheel of halloumi and a note: “Fry it, don’t boil it, and come to the gym.”

### Chapter 109: The Keeper’s Notebook

Marwan feeds the hippopotamus at dawn and writes everything down in a notebook he has kept for twenty years, under a pseudonym nobody at the zoo knows.

263. **The Keeper’s Notebook.** In the evening he tunes a piano, changes a guitar string for a student, and tells you about his thesis and the orphanage where he first learned the keys.
264. **Trial: Feed and Play.** He sends the inn a jar of honey from the oak tree and a note: “For the guests who sing badly.”

### Chapter 110: The Weather Desk

Basma reads the weather on the morning radio, for Ramallah, Gaza and Beersheba alike. She works part-time, and her afternoons go to the stories behind the places she names.

265. **The Weather Desk.** Her cousin calls from the United States. He had a nightmare, his son has head lice, and he misses the heat of home, in the high twenties.
266. **Trial: Clear Skies.** She leaves the innkeeper tomorrow’s forecast on a card: “Clear sky, about thirty. And na3ēman after your bath.”

### Chapter 111: The Mirror by the Door

Nawal keeps a salon in the back room of her house, with a mirror by the door and a pot of soup always warm in the microwave. She only got four or five hours of sleep, she says, but her scissors never stop.

267. **The Mirror by the Door.** Her husband comes in, broad-shouldered and dark-skinned, with shaving cream still under one ear. You can tell the man in the next chair wears a toupee, and Nawal says nothing at all.
268. **Trial: The Blond Question.** She sends the inn a jar of lip balm for chapped lips and a note: “For the innkeeper, who smiles too much in the wind.”

### Chapter 112: The Long Way to Dubai

Bilal sells machines for a company in Hebron and flies more than he sleeps. He comes into the inn at rush hour with a courier bag and a sunburn, and asks you to turn the TV down a bit.

269. **The Long Way to Dubai.** His flight was delayed by two hours, then he had a three-hour layover in Dubai. The airfare was reasonable, he says, and the coffee was not.
270. **Trial: Turn It Down.** He leaves a tin of sweets from Dubai on the counter and a note: “Drop me off on the corner next time. I’ll walk.”

### Chapter 113: The First Year

Ruba is in her first year at the university and lives on campus. She can get by in Arabic, she says, but her handwriting is so bad that her teachers can’t read a word.

271. **The First Year.** Her grandmother died last winter, God rest her soul. She tells you about the days of mourning, the people who came to say “God give the family patience,” and her aunt, who is due in early July.
272. **Trial: Sun and Storm.** She leaves the innkeeper a birthday card: “Many happy returns, and may you see a hundred more!”

### Chapter 114: Two Families, One Table

Lama’s father remarried last year, and this Friday both families sit down at the inn together for the first time. She is the middle child, she says, of a family that keeps growing.

273. **Two Families, One Table.** Her grandfather, an only child who lived all his life in the village, gathered a family instead. Her adopted cousin reads the year’s days from his history book: Children’s Day in March, and the days in May and November.
274. **Trial: Who Is Who.** She leaves the innkeeper a drawing of the table, every name written over every head: “So you never mix up my aunts again.”
